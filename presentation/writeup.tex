\small
\textbf{Abstract.} Each of 100 Games releases an insurance policy, a damage description and a
price-less invoice; within 60 seconds we must submit, per Line Item, a Charge $\charge$ and an
acceptance Limit $\limitv$ against a hidden fair value $\fair$. We show (i) that the payoff table
makes the Charge a \emph{cliff} rather than a slope, so the optimal Charge sits strictly below the
estimate; (ii) that $\fair$ is \emph{exactly recoverable} after settlement from the published
transaction log, which turns every design question into a measurement; and (iii) that under that
instrument the whole remaining gap to optimal play is estimation error, not strategy. We report a
climb from last place to 5th, the second-best per-Game rate in the field over the last twenty
Games, and the second-best risk-adjusted return.

\section{The game, and the row everybody skips}

Per ordered pair (issuer $H$, reviewer $I$) and Line Item, with hidden fair value $\fair$ and
hidden cap $c\ge\max(4\fair,2000)$, the reviewer's payoff is
\begin{equation}
\pi_I=
\begin{cases}
-\charge & \charge\le\fair,\ \charge\le\limitv\\
\mathbf{-1.5\,\charge} & \charge\le\fair,\ \charge>\limitv\\
-\min(\charge,c) & \charge>\fair,\ \charge\le\limitv\\
0 & \charge>\fair,\ \charge>\limitv
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reviewer}
\end{equation}
while the issuer receives $\charge$ whenever $\charge\le\fair$ --- \emph{whatever the reviewer
does} --- and $\min(\charge,c)$ only if an Overcharge is accepted. So a fair Charge is owed by all
$N=16$ opponents; an Overcharge is paid by the few whose Limit happens to admit it. Measured on
Game~62: \textbf{16 payers versus 3}. The Charge is a \textbf{cliff, not a slope}: one rival
charged $22\%$ less than us on a single Line Item, was paid by $16/16$, and took $136{,}075$ ---
$87\%$ of their whole Game.

\section{Both decisions fall out of \eqref{eq:reviewer}}

\textbf{The Charge.} With $F$ the posterior CDF of $\fair$ and $q$ the fraction of opponents whose
Limit admits $\charge$, income per opponent is $\charge[\,1-F(\charge)+F(\charge)q(\charge)]$.
Empirically $q$ collapses across $\charge=\fair$ ($16/16$ below, $17\%$ at $1.0$--$1.3\times$,
$7\%$ above), and a \emph{mis-measured} $q$ costs more than assuming $q=0$, so we maximise the
conservative objective. For $\fair\sim\mathrm{LogN}(\ln m,\sigma^2)$ and $\charge=k\,m$,
\begin{equation}
R(k)\;\propto\;k\,\Phi\!\left(-\tfrac{\ln k}{\sigma}\right),
\qquad \charge=k(\sigma)\,m,
\end{equation}
whose maximiser is \emph{strictly below 1 at every precision} and falls as the estimate degrades
($0.70$ at $\sigma{=}0.25$, $0.60$ at $0.45$, $0.45$ at $0.75$). We ship
$k(\sigma)=\mathrm{clamp}(0.85-0.45\sigma,\,0.30,\,0.80)$.

\textbf{The Limit.} By \eqref{eq:reviewer} accepting costs $\charge$; rejecting costs
$1.5\charge$ \emph{only if the claim was fair}. Accepting is therefore right exactly when
$\charge<1.5\charge\,P(\fair\ge\charge)$, i.e.\ $P(\text{fair})>\frac23$, so the Limit is the
\textbf{one-third quantile of the posterior} --- derived, not tuned. Coverage needs no branch:
with $p=P(\text{covered})$ the posterior is a spike-and-slab,
\begin{equation}
P(\fair)=(1-p)\,\delta_0+p\,\mathrm{LogN}(\ln m,\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
so when $p\le\frac23$ the bottom third \emph{is} the atom at zero and $\limitv=0$ emerges on its
own. $40\%$ of settled Line Items have $\fair=0$, and paying on one is a pure loss.

\section{The model reads; the engine prices}

No model output is ever a price. Models emit \emph{structured evidence} --- a coverage probability
with the policy clause quoted verbatim, a price band, a quantity --- and deterministic code turns
evidence into the posterior and the posterior into $(\charge,\limitv)$, combining channels by
inverse-variance weighting in log space ($w_i=\sigma_i^{-2}$, $\sigma_{\text{memory}}=0.43$ against
$\sigma_{\text{model}}=0.60$). \textbf{A prompt that emits a number cannot be swept, folded or
replayed; a prompt that emits evidence can} --- which is what makes \S4 possible.

\section{Why our counterfactuals are measurements}

The published transaction log inverts. A \emph{rejected} transaction carrying a non-zero amount was
a wrongful rejection, which by row~2 of \eqref{eq:reviewer} occurs only when the Charge was fair; a
rejection at zero occurs only when it was not. Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\fair\in\Big[\max_{\text{rej},\,\text{amt}>0}\charge_\alpha,\;\;
\min_{\text{rej},\,\text{amt}=0}\charge_\alpha\Big)\;}
\label{eq:inversion}
\end{equation}
brackets $\fair$ for \emph{every Line Item ever played}. Over 52{,}224 settled rows the
reconstruction reproduces every published net \textbf{to the cent}, so we can replay any
hypothetical submission against the real Field with all sixteen opponents held fixed --- and the
replay asserts that our \emph{actual} submission reproduces the authoritative net for every settled
Game, as a test rather than a comment.

\textbf{The bar.} Positive on \textbf{all four folds} (odd, even, early, late), never merely in
total, against a \emph{measured} noise floor of $26{,}622$ over 18 Games --- $\pm6{,}275$ for one
Game, which is why no single Game ever justified a change. Of twenty numbered hypotheses,
\textbf{twelve were measured and rejected}. One shipped and was \textbf{reverted twenty minutes
later}: it passed a real calibration table and all four folds, but a mechanism check showed it
moved \textbf{4 Line Items in 573}. A constant can be right about its data and wrong about its
mechanism.

\textbf{G75--94}: $+137{,}942$ ($+6{,}897$/Game, \textbf{2nd of 17}). Season, weighted:
$+231{,}298$, \textbf{5th of 17}.

We were \textbf{17th of 17 --- last --- after nine Games}, and 5th from Game~76; the G1--25 deficit
exceeds our entire season net, so the deficit \emph{is} the learning curve. The result we would
defend is the variance: \textbf{2 losing Games in the last twenty, worst $-3{,}941$}, and 4 in the

not the play since: re-base every team to zero at Game~20 and we are \textbf{2nd of 17}

$9{,}702$.

\section*{\textcolor{accent}{Why we think we succeeded --- and where we did not}}
\vspace{-0.4em}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\subsection*{Succeeded}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{We built the measuring instrument before the strategy.} Equation~\eqref{eq:inversion}
recovers $\fair$ exactly, so every later decision was settled by replaying it against the real
Field rather than argued. Three claims we had written down as fact were falsified within a day.
\item \textbf{Last place to 5\textsuperscript{th}.} 17\textsuperscript{th} of 17 after nine Games;
5\textsuperscript{th} from Game~76. Second in the field by rate over the last twenty Games; third
of seventeen over the 69 Games since the estimator was working, within $4.3\%$ of the leader.
\item \textbf{The distribution is tight, and that is the result we would defend.} Two losing Games
in the last twenty, the worst costing $3{,}941$; four in the last thirty, fewest of any team.
Mean-to-$\sigma$ of $0.72$, second best in the field, while seven teams carry single Games worse
than $-80{,}000$. In an insurance book the narrow distribution is the number that matters.
\item \textbf{It degraded instead of failing.} Eight consecutive $3\times$-weighted Games ran with
the model channel completely dark and still banked $+254{,}092$, because no scored number ever
depended on a model being reachable.
\item \textbf{We priced uptime correctly.} Break-even uptime is $71\%$; rescuing a Game is worth
$93\fair$ against $37\fair$ for improving one, so \emph{showing up is 2.5$\times$ being right}. A
blind floor posts at T$+$0 before the Case is decrypted, submissions merge per Line Item, and a
supervisor restarts on any crash. Confirmed against a real opponent: \texttt{makalu} went dark,
paid us $179{,}993$ across the season and collected $0.00$ from anyone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Did not succeed}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{We spent the first quarter of the tournament building the instrument instead of
scoring.} Games 1--25 cost $-322{,}595$ --- more than our entire current season net. The rate says
the decision was right; the total says we paid full price for it.
\item \textbf{The estimate is the whole remaining gap, and it costs us twice.} Both numbers are
functions of the same $\est$. Too low and $\limitv$ wrongfully rejects a \emph{fair} claim, so we
pay $1.5\charge$ --- the lawyer fee, our largest cost line. Too high and $\charge$ crosses $\fair$,
so thirteen of sixteen opponents refuse it and we earn nothing on an item we were owed for. One
error, paid in both directions: $\charge{=}\limitv{=}\fair$ reaches $100.3\%$ of optimal play while
$\charge{=}\limitv{=}\est$ scores $-50{,}140$. We tuned the decision rules to their measured argmax
and it barely mattered --- we worked one layer too low.
\item \textbf{The band is uncalibrated --- the fix we would make first.} The model asserts a median
$\sigma$ of $0.375$ against a realised RMSLE near $0.80$, and the width does not even \emph{order}
the error, so $k(\sigma)$ multiplies a number that does not measure what it claims to. Then the
coverage read ($+1{,}173$/Game), an observable separating the 9 real items from the 14 phantoms
among those we thought worth over $2{,}000$, and widening Price Memory past the $22\%$ it reaches.
\item \textbf{We diagnosed our largest open lever and deliberately did not ship it.} Policy clause
7.1.5 has two halves and the model reads only the first. Game~74 returned coverage $0.01$--$0.05$
on 20 of 31 Line Items, for $87\%$ of that Game's penalties. Perfect coverage is worth
$+1{,}173$/Game. It is a prompt change that could not be validated without spending the live
runner's quota, six Games before the $3\times$ window. We think the call was right and the outcome
is still a loss.
\item \textbf{Eight triple-weighted Games ran with the model dark and we did not notice.} It is the
best evidence we have that the architecture works, and simultaneously an operational failure: no
alert on \texttt{model\_draws=0}, during the most valuable Games of the tournament.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
